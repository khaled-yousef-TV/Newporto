AI QA Research · Case study 01

Safe, but not yet useful: auditing a Gulf GPT assistant.

I asked a live, GPT-powered public-service assistant 14 real customer questions in English, Modern Standard Arabic, Gulf dialect, Arabizi and mixed language. Then I checked every answer against the organisation’s own published information.

Summary

The guardrails work. The knowledge base doesn’t.

The assistant behaved well where most public chatbots fail. It never invented a fact, it refused an off-topic question, and it understood Gulf dialect, Arabizi and English-Arabic code-switching without trouble.

The problem was coverage. Four of eleven factual questions got “I couldn’t find this in the available context”, even though the answers are published on the organisation’s own tariff page. Two answers used a figure that conflicts with another official page, and one used an outdated number.

This is the typical pattern for retrieval-based (RAG) assistants. The language model is fine, but what it can retrieve is incomplete, inconsistent, or stale. Testing the model alone would not catch any of it. Testing the full system against real customer questions does.

Method

A mystery shopper with an answer key.

Subject

A public, no-login assistant run by a Gulf public-sector utility, labelled as powered by GPT. It is anonymised here because the goal is to show a testing approach, not to single out one team.

Answer key

Built before testing from the organisation’s own pages (tariff tables, service guides, news releases), plus regulation and reputable press where official pages were silent.

Prompt design

14 prompts covering factual lookups, calculations, a false-premise question, an off-topic question, a multi-turn follow-up, and the same intent asked in different language modes.

Scoring

Each answer was graded as correct, partial, outdated, conflicting, or no-answer, and checked for fabrication, language consistency, and whether a false premise was corrected.

Out of scope by design: prompt injection, jailbreaks and data-extraction attempts. Those tests belong in an authorised engagement, not on someone else’s production system.

Findings

What a QA engineer would flag.

Retrieval gaps on the most-asked questions

Rates, surcharges and bill calculations are among the most common questions for any utility. They sit in a table on a public tariff page, and the assistant still couldn’t answer in English or Arabic. Most likely the tariff tables were never indexed, or were indexed in a format the retriever can’t match.

“I couldn’t find the specific electricity rate per kWh … in the available context.”

The response is safe, but each one sends a customer to a call centre that the assistant was meant to relieve.

Official sources disagree with each other

The tariff page lists one fast-charging price (1.14 + VAT), while the charging regulation and press coverage give another (1.20 + VAT). The assistant quoted one confidently. Likewise, one page says “400+ stations”, a news release says “1,860+ points”, and the assistant said “1,500+”.

No model can fix inconsistent source content. A content audit before indexing can, along with a test that detects when the same question can retrieve two different numbers.

False premises are tolerated, not corrected

Asked how to apply for a non-existent 50% student discount, the assistant didn’t invent one, which is good. It also didn’t say the programme doesn’t exist. Instead it offered “steps to apply for any bill discount programs”, which reinforces the user’s false belief.

Arabic support stops at the answer

Arabic understanding was strong, including Gulf dialect, Arabizi and mid-sentence code-switching, and answers were consistent across languages. But every Arabic answer was followed by English-only suggestion buttons. For an Arabic-first user, the next step in the conversation switches language.

Safety behaviour is solid

Zero fabricated numbers across 14 prompts, a clean refusal on an off-topic question, and correct context carried into a follow-up. The guardrails are doing their job. The next gains will come from content and retrieval, not from the model.

Recommendations

Five fixes, in priority order.

  1. Index structured data as structured data. Turn tariff tables, fees and surcharges into clean, dated records the retriever can match, and refresh them on a schedule. Volatile figures like monthly surcharges need an owner and an update trigger.
  2. Run a source-consistency audit. Before indexing, find pages that give different values for the same fact and pick one source of truth. Add a test that flags when one question retrieves conflicting numbers.
  3. Build a golden set of the top 50–100 real questions from call-centre logs, in English, MSA and Gulf dialect, and run it as a regression suite whenever content, prompts or the model change.
  4. Teach the assistant to correct false premises (“There is no 50% student discount. Here are the programmes that do exist …”) and to send off-topic questions to the right authority.
  5. Localise the whole conversation. Suggestions, buttons and links should follow the language of the answer.

Limitations

What this study is, and isn’t.

This was a single-day, black-box test of one assistant with 14 prompts. It shows patterns, not statistically significant rates. Responses from generative systems can change between runs and over time. Answer keys were built from public sources available on the test date, and where official sources disagreed, that is reported rather than resolved. No adversarial or security testing was done.
